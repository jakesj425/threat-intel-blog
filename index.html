1. Three reports dropped today, and the standout is Operation DragonReturn — a China-nexus campaign targeting India's Ministry of Finance tax infrastructure that's been running since mid-May and is still active with a 0/66 VT detection rate as recently as June 12. Seqrite's writeup is thorough, and I'll get to the technical details, but first I want to flag what this campaign actually means for defenders.

2. DragonReturn is exploiting the AY2026-27 ITR filing season in India by cloning legitimate Income Tax Department utility filenames and embedding DcRAT through a multi-stage chain that includes steganographic extraction from background.jpg, AMSI bypass, and Windows service persistence disguised as "Windows Mixed Reality Service." The C2 infrastructure resolves to AS4134 (ChinaNet — Jiangxi province) and AS152194 (CTG Server). Seqrite attributes this to a Silver Fox overlap with medium-high confidence, and the Chinese-language artifacts in the infrastructure panels back that up. What makes this operationally interesting is the payload rotation cadence — every 7-10 days, fresh variants. That's not a spray-and-pray operation. Someone is actively maintaining this and adapting to detection feedback.

3. For anyone in Indian finance, tax preparation, or adjacent sectors: treat any unsolicited email referencing ITR filings with extreme skepticism right now. The lure quality here is high — real legal citations, bilingual formatting consistent with GoI communications, and a fake Assistant Commissioner as signatory. Your security awareness training probably doesn't cover this level of impersonation fidelity. Block govtop[.]one at the DNS level and review any .zip downloads claiming to be tax utilities.

4. The Microsoft report on the hospitality Node.js campaign is the other big one this cycle. Seventy obfuscation phases across two waves, authentication laundering through Calendly's SendGrid infrastructure and Google's open redirect — that's the kind of operational maturity that makes attribution harder and detection trickier. The dual persistence model (RunOnce for PE + Run for Node.js) is a detail worth remembering for IR. If you clean one but miss the other, the implant resurrects itself and re-downloads payloads. I've seen this pattern before in pre-ransomware access broker campaigns, though Microsoft hasn't attributed this one yet. Watch for node.exe running from AppData\Local\Nodejs\ with a .js argument — that's unusual in any non-development environment.

5. The Langflow CVE piece from Sysdig is a shorter read but maybe the most useful for vulnerability management teams. CVE-2026-55255 scores 9.9 (IDOR, cross-tenant) while CVE-2026-33017 scores 9.3 (unauthenticated RCE). Guess which one is in CISA KEV and has ~7,000 servers under attack? The lower-scored RCE. The IDOR requires auth and a valid flow UUID you can't guess, so it's practically inert unless paired with a disclosure bug (which the operator found by probing /api/v1/flows/ first). The lesson isn't new but bears repeating: CVSS base score measures severity, not exploitation likelihood. If you're prioritizing patches purely by CVSS, you're prioritizing wrong. The unauthenticated, internet-sprayable vuln with the lower score is what's burning you.

Source: sysdig.com

6. The Friday backlog (Cisco SD-WAN 0-day CVE-2026-20245, AsyncRAT/Remcos phishing campaign, Prinz Eugen ransomware, BabaDeda loader, KimJongRAT, Millenium RAT) mostly rounds out what was already a busy week. Nothing in there that changes the risk posture, but the Cisco SD-WAN exploitation is worth a note if you run Catalyst SD-WAN Manager — Google's TAG documented active zero-day exploitation, so check your exposure.

Source: cloud.google.com

7. TL;DR: DragonReturn is the actionable priority if you touch Indian financial systems — this is live, well-resourced espionage. The hospitality Node.js campaign matters for its operational tradecraft and the persistence lesson. And for VM teams: the Langflow story is a case study in why CVSS-driven prioritization is dangerous.

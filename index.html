Opinion of a DFIR Guy

TL;DR
  • 15 reports analyzed — 4 Malware Loaders & Backdoors, 4 Credential Theft & Infostealers, 3 Supply Chain Attacks
  • 5 adversarial groups identified
  • 31 CVEs in active use
  • Nation-state campaigns in 1 reports
  • Top report: npm Packages Hijacked in Supply Chain Attack

The headline this cycle isn't a nation-state, a zero-day, or a new ransomware gang. It's two separate threat actors independently deciding that an Ethereum smart contract is the right place to hang their command-and-control domains. Wiz, Netskope, and Expel are all tracking the Shai-Hulud / ChainDrop npm worm rolling through the `keyv` and `cacheable` ecosystem, and at the same time Hunt.io is breaking down The Gentlemen's EtherRAT. Two unrelated campaigns, two different payloads, one shared quirk: the C2 hostname is resolved at runtime by an `eth_call` to a blockchain contract instead of being hardcoded.

Source: hunt.io

That crossover is the only thing in this entire pulse dump I'd call genuinely novel. The operationally interesting bit for the Shai-Hulud wave is that the worm pulls its exfil domain (`npm-cache[.]com`) from contract state through public Ethereum RPC providers. The operator can re-point the entire credential harvest to new infrastructure without touching a single published package. Defenders can't flag what a static scan won't find — `Math_Symbol.js` ships no binary, hardcodes no C2, downloads a legitimately-signed Bun runtime, runs, then deletes it. The tarball on disk is clean. That's the point where you stop trusting file hashes and start trusting process behavior.

What frustrates me is how much of this was avoidable on the hygiene side. ChainDrop started with a compromised GitHub maintainer account pushing code straight to main, so the maintainer's own Actions pipelines built, signed, and published the poisoned versions. For the second time in a year this worm family's propagation loop runs on stolen npm publish tokens. Before anyone debates the clever Ethereum C2, the real lesson is that CI/CD was holding publishing rights that don't survive account compromise. Lock down publish rights, use short-lived scoped tokens, and actually read your Actions logs.

Elsewhere the RMM abuse theme keeps grinding. ScreenConnect shows up three separate ways — Huntress's fake Bank of America lure with a `AccountGuardSetup.vbs` → base64-on-base64 → AES → ICMLuaUtil UAC bypass chain, Securonix's SMOKE#SCREEN with rotating payload hashes and a `// WAIT 3 MINUTES (Breaks Elastic correlation)` comment in the loader source, and Kaspersky's ScreenConnect-masked-as-freeware operation behind spoofed OBS/DNS Jumper/DS4Windows sites. That comment is the detail that sticks. An attacker explicitly tuning `Thread.Sleep(180000)` to break EDR correlation windows, then rotating hashes to kill hash-based detection, means we're working a live arms race, not spraying spam.

The Gentlemen report is the most complete view of a ransomware affiliate's toolkit I've seen in a single pulse: EtherRAT plus Sliver, bare Go reverse shells, Mimikatz, Chisel, the Potato-family priv-esc chain, and scheduled-task lateral movement masquerading as OS updaters (WinSvcUpdate2, WindowsUpdSvc). If you want a detection, hunt for `schtasks /create` running under SYSTEM plus `certutil.exe` fetching an MSI from a raw IP.

Source: hunt.io

The rest of the cycle is more of the same-old: fake AI-tool installers, an npm hijack across enterprise namespaces, the QuickFox VPN supply-chain FDMTP implant with process-based guardrails, XCSSET v4, MacSync, Phantom Stealer, and the usual cred-phish and stealer noise. The genuinely interesting stuff is all in the infrastructure: the Shai-Hulud wave, EtherRAT's contract-resolved C2, and ScreenConnect relay clusters compartmentalized with three independent RSA key pairs so one burned relay leaves the rest alive.

Sources: wiz.io, netskope.com, expel.com

And there's the defender's silver lining. Blockchain writes are permanent. Those on-chain C2 updates are a forensic gift — you can reconstruct the entire historical domain rotation from the ledger. Netskope's function selector `0x02d1e413` and the Shai-Hulud contract are durable indicators that survive C2 rotation. I've already pulled five C2s off EtherRAT's contract that no static sample would ever surface. Use that.

Sources: wiz.io, netskope.com, expel.com
